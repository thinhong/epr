3  Bayesian

Every method so far has answered “how high is too high” by looking up comparable weeks and asking how far above them this week sits. This one answers it differently. It writes down a full probability distribution for what next week’s count could be, then cuts that distribution at a high quantile and calls everything above the cut a signal.

The difference lives entirely in the tail. A distribution built from parameters you pretend to know is narrower than one built from parameters you admit you had to estimate, and a narrower distribution puts the threshold lower than it should be. Raise the uncertainty slider below and watch the two thresholds come apart.

The algorithm is BODA, the Bayesian Outbreak Detection Algorithm of Manitz and Höhle (Manitz and Höhle 2013), implemented in R’s surveillance package as boda() (Salmon et al. 2016). Salmon and colleagues later extended it to handle reporting delay (Salmon et al. 2015), which is bodaDelay().

3.1 What a reference set leaves behind

Before the method, the complaint it answers. Reference-value methods — the CUSUM and RKI chapters both belong here — pay for their simplicity in four ways.

They use a sliver of the data. A handful of weeks from a handful of years decides the threshold. The rest of the series still carries information about trend and seasonality, and none of it is read.

The answer depends on a window you chose. How many years back, how many weeks either side. Section 2.1.1 showed those choices fixing the size of the comparison before any data is read, and nothing in the method tells you which choice is right.

They act as though the estimate were the truth. The reference mean is substituted straight into the predictive distribution, as if it were known rather than estimated. This is the plug-in step, and it is what narrows the distribution and drops the threshold.

There is nowhere to put a cause. Cases rise for reasons — weather, population movement, a change in who gets tested. A reference set has no slot for any of them.

One clarification is worth making before going further, because the chapter title invites the wrong reading. The line that separates these two families is not frequentist against Bayesian. It is reference values against a regression model. The Bayesian framework by itself fixes only the third complaint, the plug-in step. The other three fall only because the method moves to a full regression on the whole series.

The package makes the point for you. surveillance also ships algo.bayes, which is Bayesian and still reference-value based — and still has all three of the other problems.

3.2 Computing an epidemic threshold with BODA

boda() runs in six steps. The first three build a model, the fourth fits it, and the last two turn the fit into a number you can compare a count against.

1. Choose the distribution. Case counts almost always vary more than their own mean, because transmission clusters and so the observations are not independent. Section 2.3 made that case in full; BODA takes it as the starting point and uses a negative binomial throughout:

\[ y_t \mid \mu_t, \nu \;\sim\; \mathrm{NB}(\mu_t, \nu), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(y_t) = \mu_t + \frac{\mu_t^2}{\nu}. \]

Watch the direction of \(\nu\), which is easy to read backwards. A larger \(\nu\) means less spread; as \(\nu \to \infty\) the distribution collapses to Poisson. It measures the inverse of overdispersion.

2. Model the expected count. Everything that should move the expectation goes into a linear predictor on the log scale:

\[ \log \mu_t \;=\; \log P_t + \alpha_{0t} + \beta t + \gamma_t + X_t^{\!\top}\delta + \zeta z_t. \]

Each term has one job. \(\log P_t\) is a population offset, so the model works on rates rather than raw counts and districts of different sizes can be compared. \(\alpha_{0t}\) is an intercept free to drift, smoothing the baseline level. \(\beta t\) is long-run trend. \(\gamma_t\) is the seasonal cycle, period 52 for weekly data. \(X_t^{\!\top}\delta\) is where covariates enter — temperature, absolute humidity, rainfall, usually lagged. And \(\zeta z_t\) marks weeks already flagged as aberrant, so that old epidemic peaks are not learned as ordinary baseline.

That last term deserves a second look, because it sounds like the objection raised against reference methods: doesn’t somebody have to decide in advance which weeks were epidemic? No. In the implementation z_t is not supplied by the user. With pastAberrations = TRUE, it is read from the alarms the algorithm itself raised at earlier time points. The loop closes on its own.

3. Put a prior on everything. In a Bayesian model every parameter is a random variable and needs a distribution before the data arrives. This model is a latent Gaussian model, which splits the parameters into two tiers: a latent field \(x = (\alpha_{0t}, \beta, \gamma_t, \delta, \zeta)\) of regression coefficients and random effects, assumed jointly Gaussian; and a small set of hyperparameters \(\theta = (\nu, \tau)\) controlling dispersion and how smooth each effect is.

The split is not bookkeeping. Keeping the hyperparameters few — under about fifteen — is the condition that makes step 4 possible (Rue et al. 2009).

In practice: the drifting intercept is declared through f(time, model = ...), where rw1 (a first-order random walk, a slowly wandering baseline) is a sensible default for weekly data, with iid and rw2 as the looser and smoother alternatives. Setting season = TRUE gives the seasonal term a cyclic spline, so the end of one year joins the start of the next. Fixed coefficients get vague Gaussian priors and let the data speak. For the hyperparameters INLA defaults to log-Gamma; on short series or thin ward-level counts, a PC prior is less sensitive to how you set it. Run the whole thing under at least two prior choices and check the answer does not move.

4. Fit it. With weeks \(1\) to \(T-1\) in hand, Bayes’ theorem gives

\[ p(x, \theta \mid y_{1:T-1}) \;\propto\; p(y_{1:T-1} \mid x, \theta)\, p(x \mid \theta)\, p(\theta). \]

The normalising constant is an integral with no closed form. MCMC would sample it accurately and take hours for one series — impossible for a system that runs weekly across hundreds of units. BODA uses INLA instead (Rue et al. 2009), a deterministic approximation built for exactly this class of model, which matches MCMC closely and runs orders of magnitude faster.

5. Average over what you do not know. The parameters were never the point. What is wanted is \(y_T\), and the distribution for it integrates the model away:

\[ p(y_T \mid y_{1:T-1}) \;=\; \int p(y_T \mid x, \theta)\, p(x, \theta \mid y_{1:T-1})\, \mathrm{d}x\, \mathrm{d}\theta. \]

This single line is the whole argument of the chapter. It folds together two different uncertainties: counts bounce around even when the parameters are known exactly, and the parameters are not known exactly. Plug-in methods carry only the first.

One implementation detail follows directly from step 4 and is easy to get wrong. INLA returns the marginal posterior of each parameter, not their joint distribution. boda() therefore takes a samplingMethod argument, and it should be set to 'joint'. Sampling each marginal independently discards the correlations between parameters and narrows the predictive distribution — reintroducing, by the back door, the exact defect the method exists to remove.

6. Cut the tail. The threshold is the \((1-\alpha)\) quantile of that predictive distribution. Counts are whole numbers, so the honest definition is the smallest integer whose cumulative probability reaches \(1-\alpha\):

\[ \xi_{1-\alpha} \;=\; \min\Bigl\{ k \in \{0,1,2,\dots\} \;:\; \textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{k} p(y_T = j \mid y_{1:T-1}) \;\ge\; 1-\alpha \Bigr\}. \]

A week signals when \(y_T^{\text{obs}} > \xi_{1-\alpha}\).

3.2.1 Why the Bayesian threshold sits higher

The claim that the predictive distribution is wider can be made exact. Split its variance by conditioning on the parameters first:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(y_T \mid \text{data}) \;=\; \operatorname{E}\!\bigl[\operatorname{Var}(y_T \mid \mu)\bigr] \;+\; \operatorname{Var}\!\bigl(\operatorname{E}[y_T \mid \mu]\bigr). \]

The first term is how much the counts would scatter if you knew where they were centred. The second is how much the centre itself could move.

Substitute the negative binomial, for which \(\operatorname{Var}(y \mid \mu) = \mu + \mu^2/\nu\) and \(\operatorname{E}[y \mid \mu] = \mu\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(y_T \mid \text{data}) \;=\; \operatorname{E}[\mu] + \frac{\operatorname{E}[\mu^2]}{\nu} + \operatorname{Var}(\mu). \]

Write \(\hat\mu = \operatorname{E}[\mu]\) and \(s^2 = \operatorname{Var}(\mu)\), and use \(\operatorname{E}[\mu^2] = \hat\mu^2 + s^2\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(y_T \mid \text{data}) \;=\; \underbrace{\hat\mu + \frac{\hat\mu^2}{\nu}}_{\text{plug-in}} \;+\; s^2\Bigl(1 + \frac{1}{\nu}\Bigr). \]

The brace is exactly what a plug-in method computes. Everything after it is what a plug-in method discards, and it has a shape worth reading. The penalty grows with the square of how uncertain the expected count is, so it stays negligible until the estimate is genuinely shaky and then grows fast. And it is larger when \(\nu\) is small: the more overdispersed the counts already are, the more parameter uncertainty piles on top.

Figure 3.1 opens at \(\hat\mu = 15\), \(\nu = 6\) and 30% uncertainty. The plug-in variance is 52.50, the formula adds about 24.7, and the total is 77.17. In cases, the plug-in threshold is 28 and the Bayesian one is 32 — and a threshold of 28 that advertises a 5% false alarm rate delivers 7.6%.

3.3 When this week’s count is not yet this week’s count

For ward-level surveillance this is not a refinement. It is the main problem.

A week’s count is never finished when the week is. Cases have to present, be tested, be confirmed and be entered, and all of that takes time. So at the moment the algorithm runs, the current week is under-reported — while the threshold it is compared against was calibrated on historical weeks that have long since filled in. The comparison is between an incomplete number and a complete-number threshold, and it fails in the worst possible direction: outbreaks are missed precisely when detecting them matters most.

Set the wait to zero and read the figure as reported: only about a third of each week’s cases exist yet, and all three genuine exceedances slip under the threshold. Wait two weeks and all three are caught — but every alarm now arrives two weeks after the week it describes, which for a vector-borne outbreak is most of the window in which a response would have helped.

The second tab is the way out. If you know what share of a week’s cases has usually arrived by a given delay, you can scale the partial count back up and judge the week immediately. That is what delay correction does, and at a wait of zero it recovers all three.

Salmon and colleagues built this into the model rather than bolting it on (Salmon et al. 2015). The data are treated as a two-way table — week of onset against reporting delay — with the lower right corner missing, and that table is modelled with negative binomial regression. The implementation is bodaDelay(), and using it means preparing a reporting triangle rather than a plain weekly series.

Worth knowing before you plan around it: bodaDelay() combines the Manitz–Höhle algorithm with Noufaily’s (Noufaily et al. 2013), and so it still takes reference-window arguments b and w. The dependence on a chosen window, the second complaint in Section 3.1, is therefore not fully removed. That is a deliberate trade of theoretical tidiness for something that runs.

3.4 Practical notes

The software is experimental. boda() says so in its own documentation. It depends on INLA, which is not on CRAN and installs from its own repository:

install.packages("INLA", repos = "https://inla.r-inla-download.org/R/stable")

It is slow, because a Bayesian model is fitted at every time point. And results are not reproducible under INLA’s default parallelism; for a reproducible run, set INLA::inla.setOption(num.threads = "1:1") before set.seed().

Many units at once is a different problem. This is the easiest thing to overlook when moving from one series to a whole city. Run the algorithm across \(N\) units at level \(\alpha\) and, with no outbreak anywhere, the expected number of false alarms per week is \(N\alpha\). For 168 units at \(\alpha = 0.05\) that is about eight false alarms every week — enough to exhaust a response team’s trust within a few months. The options are to lower \(\alpha\) and accept the loss of sensitivity, to control a false discovery rate rather than each alarm separately, or to require a signal to repeat across weeks or cluster in space before it triggers fieldwork. Whichever you pick, write down why.

Use the population offset. When units differ greatly in size, \(\log P_t\) has to enter the model as an offset. Without it, dense wards carry permanently high thresholds and sparse ones alarm constantly, and neither reflects real risk. Check whether the version of boda() you are running accepts an offset, since its X argument takes covariates as fixed effects only.

3.5 Measuring whether it works

A detection algorithm is worth only what it can be shown to do. The minimum set: sensitivity (the share of real outbreaks detected), specificity and the false alarm rate, and timeliness — an algorithm that detects everything four weeks late detects nothing useful. Because the output here is a whole distribution rather than a yes-or-no verdict, add a proper scoring rule, which grades the probability forecast itself.

Evaluate on both simulated data, where the true outbreaks are known by construction, and on real historical series, which is how Manitz and Höhle assessed BODA (Manitz and Höhle 2013). Unkel and colleagues give a fuller comparison framework (Unkel et al. 2012).
