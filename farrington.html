4  Farrington

Farrington asks the same question as RKI: is this week high for this time of year? RKI averages comparable weeks from past years. Farrington fits a regression line through them instead, so the expected count can follow a trend, and the scatter of ordinary weeks is measured, not assumed. It was built in England and Wales to screen weekly laboratory reports (Farrington et al. 1996), and Salmon et al. (2016) call it the method of choice at European public health institutes.

4.1 Computing an epidemic threshold with Farrington

The method runs in four steps, and each one has a figure to play with.

4.1.1 Step 1: choose comparable weeks

Counts rise and fall with the seasons, so this week is compared only with the same time of year in past years. If this is week \(x\) of year \(y\), the baseline is weeks \(x-w\) to \(x+w\) of the years \(y-b\) to \(y-1\): \(b\) years back, \(w\) weeks either side. That gives

\[ n = b\,(2w+1) \]

baseline weeks. Farrington and colleagues used \(b = 5\) and \(w = 3\), so \(n = 35\).

This choice of weeks is how the method handles seasonality, so the model needs no seasonal term. A wider window, or more years, gives more weeks to learn from. But a wide window reaches into another part of the season, and old years may reflect old reporting habits or laboratory methods (Farrington et al. 1996). The current year is never used, so an outbreak in progress cannot raise its own baseline.

4.1.2 Step 2: fit a line through the windows

Each baseline count \(y_i\), in week \(t_i\), has mean \(\mu_i\) and variance \(\varphi\mu_i\). This is a quasi-Poisson model: a Poisson model in which the variance may be larger than the mean. The only systematic effect is a straight-line trend on the log scale:

\[ \log \mu_i = \alpha + \beta\, t_i. \]

Weeks are counted from the first baseline week, so the baseline weeks are \(t = 1\) to \(7\), \(53\) to \(59\), and so on, and this week is \(t_0 = 264\). The line carried forward to \(t_0\) gives the expected count, \(\hat\mu_0 = \exp(\hat\alpha + \hat\beta\, t_0)\). The scatter around the line gives the dispersion, with Poisson scatter as the floor (Section 2.3):

\[ \hat\varphi = \max\left\{1,\ \frac{1}{n-p}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \omega_i\, \frac{(y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2}{\hat\mu_i}\right\}, \]

where \(p\) is 2 with the trend and 1 without. Every weight \(\omega_i\) is 1 until step 4.

The trend is kept only if all three checks pass (Farrington et al. 1996):

  1. the baseline spans at least 3 years;
  2. \(\beta\) is significant at the 5% level;
  3. \(\hat\mu_0\) is no larger than the largest baseline count.

The third check stops a steep line from running away when it is carried a year past the data. If any check fails, the model is fitted again as a flat line.

4.1.3 Step 3: set the threshold

The threshold \(U\) is the top of a prediction interval: the range this week’s count should fall in if nothing unusual is happening. It allows for the scatter of the count (\(\hat\varphi\hat\mu_0\)) and for the uncertainty in \(\hat\mu_0\). It is built on the \(2/3\) power scale, where counts are close to symmetric:

\[ U = \hat\mu_0\left(1 + \frac{2}{3}\, z\, \sqrt{\frac{\hat\varphi\hat\mu_0 + \operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_0)}{\hat\mu_0^{2}}}\,\right)^{3/2}. \]

By default farrington() uses \(z = 1.96\), the top of a two-sided 95% interval (Salmon et al. 2016). The week raises an alarm when its count is above \(U\), unless fewer than 5 cases were reported in the last 4 weeks (Farrington et al. 1996).

4.1.4 Step 4: discount old outbreaks

An old outbreak in the baseline pulls the line up and inflates \(\hat\varphi\), so the threshold rises and the next outbreak is harder to see. Farrington therefore fits the model again, with less weight on weeks far above the line. Each week gets a standardised residual \(s_i\) (an Anscombe residual: its distance from the line on the \(2/3\) power scale, divided by its standard deviation), and

\[ \omega_i = \begin{cases} \gamma\, s_i^{-2}, & s_i > c,\\ \gamma, & s_i \le c, \end{cases} \]

where \(\gamma\) makes the weights add up to \(n\). Only weeks above the line can lose weight. In the package, this refit comes straight after the first fit, before the trend checks and the threshold.

The cut-off \(c\) decides how much of the past is believed. For ordinary weeks \(s_i\) is roughly standard normal, so the 1996 cut-off \(c = 1\) also shrinks about 1 ordinary week in 6. Those weeks carry the ordinary scatter, so \(\hat\varphi\) and the threshold fall: choose No outbreak and c = 1 to see it. Noufaily et al. (2013) raised the cut-off to \(c = 2.58\), which touches about 1 ordinary week in 200. In a 35-week baseline, though, 2.58 can miss much of an old outbreak: the outbreak inflates \(\hat\varphi\), and a larger \(\hat\varphi\) shrinks every residual, including its own. Noufaily and colleagues paired the higher cut-off with a much longer baseline.

4.2 The 2013 revision

Noufaily et al. (2013) kept the four steps and changed the settings to reduce false alarms.

In a test on weekly counts of more than 2000 diseases, the false alarm rate fell from 1.63% to below 0.9%. The share of outbreaks detected also fell, from 57% to 51% (negative binomial) and 53% (quasi-Poisson) (Enki et al. 2016). Public Health England replaced the original algorithm with the negative binomial version.

4.3 Running it in R

Both versions are in R’s surveillance package (Salmon et al. 2016).

library(tidyverse)
library(surveillance)

# weekly: one row per week, with columns date (a Date) and cases
cases <- sts(observed = weekly$cases, epoch = weekly$date, frequency = 52)

res96 <- cases |> farrington(control = list(b = 5, w = 3, alpha = 0.05))

res13 <- cases |> farringtonFlexible(control = list(
  b = 5, w = 3,
  noPeriods = 10,               # 10-level seasonal factor
  pastWeeksNotIncluded = 26,
  pThresholdTrend = 1,          # no significance test for the trend
  weightsThreshold = 2.58,
  thresholdMethod = "nbPlugin",
  alpha = 0.05                  # one-sided in this function
))

autoplot(res13)                 # counts, threshold and alarms (ggplot2)

Two things catch people out:

  • alpha is two-sided in farrington() but one-sided in farringtonFlexible(), so the same value gives a lower threshold, and more alarms, in the newer function.
  • The defaults of farringtonFlexible() are neither version: the 1996 baseline, trend test and threshold, with the 2013 cut-off of 2.58. A misspelled name in control is ignored without a warning, so copy the names exactly.

4.4 What an alarm means

An alarm means this week’s count is higher than the model predicted for this time of year, at the chosen error rate. It is a prompt to check, not a confirmed outbreak: at a one-sided 5% level, 100 series give about 5 chance alarms every week. The design also has limits:

  • It judges each week alone, so a slow, sustained rise can slip through. CUSUM adds up that evidence.
  • It assumes the season comes at the same time every year. An early or late season is compared with the wrong weeks.
  • It has no place for covariates (other drivers of the counts, such as rainfall). BODA adds them (Section 3.2).
