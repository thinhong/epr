4  Farrington

The RKI chapter judged a week by averaging a handful of comparable weeks. Farrington asks the same question — is this week high for this week of the year — but answers it by fitting a model to those weeks instead of averaging them. A regression line gives a baseline that can rise or fall over the years, one shared dispersion parameter says how much ordinary weeks scatter around it, and a right-skewed prediction interval supplies the threshold.

That buys one thing a reference set cannot: the method can notice that some of its own reference weeks were themselves epidemic, and discount them. How harshly it should discount them is the question the whole method turns on, and the two published versions answer it differently.

The method comes from the Communicable Disease Surveillance Centre in England and Wales, where it was built to screen weekly laboratory reports across more than three thousand organisms at once (Farrington et al. 1996). That setting shaped it: one formula, no per-disease tuning, working equally on an organism seen a few times a year and one seen hundreds of times a week. Noufaily and colleagues revised it seventeen years later after simulation showed the false alarm rate was too high (Noufaily et al. 2013).

4.1 Which weeks count as comparable

Both versions start from the same window. For the week under assessment, take the same calendar week in each of the \(b\) previous years, plus \(w\) weeks either side:

\[ R \;=\; \bigl\{\, t_0 - 52i + j \;:\; i = 1,\dots,b;\ j = -w,\dots,+w \,\bigr\}, \qquad n = b(2w+1). \]

With the package defaults \(b = 5\) and \(w = 3\), that is 35 weeks. Seasonality is handled by the choice of window rather than by a term in the model: only weeks sitting at the same point in the year are ever compared, so no sine and cosine are needed. The current year is excluded, so an outbreak in progress cannot inflate its own baseline.

The cost is visible as soon as you draw it.

Thirty-five weeks out of the 260 in five years is 13% of the data. The other 87% is not merely unused for the baseline — it is unavailable for estimating the trend and the dispersion too, which is where a thin reference set hurts most.

The 2013 revision keeps the window but stops discarding everything else. It adds a ten-level seasonal factor: the window itself is the reference level, and the remaining weeks of the year are divided into nine blocks, which fit exactly since \(7 + 9 \times 5 = 52\). Each block gets its own multiplier \(e^{\delta_j}\). Weeks outside the window are now explained by their block rather than thrown away, so they contribute to \(\beta\) and \(\varphi\) without dragging the current window’s level around.

Two further changes come with it. The 26 weeks immediately before \(t_0\) are held out, so a new outbreak a few weeks old cannot teach the model to expect itself — the 1996 version needed no such rule, because its windows already sat the better part of a year away. The arithmetic gives

\[ n = 52b + w - 26 = 237 \]

reference weeks at the defaults, against 35. The third change comes free with it: with 200-odd points behind the slope, the trend no longer has to pass a significance test to be kept (Noufaily et al. 2013).

4.2 Computing an epidemic threshold with Farrington

1. Fit a quasi-Poisson regression. On the reference counts,

\[ \log \mu_i = \alpha + \beta t_i, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(y_i) = \varphi\,\mu_i. \]

Here \(\alpha\) is the baseline level on the log scale and \(\beta\) the weekly change, so \(e^{\beta}\) is a weekly multiplier. The quasi-Poisson family is there because surveillance counts are almost always overdispersed; Section 2.3 sets out what happens when that is ignored. In the 2013 version the seasonal factor joins the linear predictor as \(\log \mu_i = \theta + \beta t_i + \delta_{j(i)}\).

2. Estimate the dispersion, with a floor at 1. From the weighted Pearson statistic,

\[ \varphi = \max\left\{1,\; \frac{1}{n-p}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \omega_i \frac{(y_i - \mu_i)^2}{\mu_i}\right\}, \]

where \(p\) is the number of regression parameters and \(\omega_i = 1\) on the first pass. The floor matters: \(\varphi < 1\) would mean “less variable than Poisson”, which on 35 points is nearly always estimation noise, so Poisson variance is taken as the minimum.

3. Downweight the weeks that look like past outbreaks. For each reference week compute a standardised Anscombe residual,

\[ s_i \;=\; \frac{y_i^{2/3} - \mu_i^{2/3}}{\tfrac{2}{3}\sqrt{\varphi}\;\mu_i^{1/6}\sqrt{1 - h_{ii}}}, \]

then assign weights and fit once more:

\[ \omega_i = \begin{cases} \gamma / s_i^2, & s_i > c,\\ \gamma, & s_i \le c, \end{cases} \qquad \gamma \text{ chosen so that } \textstyle\sum_i \omega_i = n. \]

The cut-off is \(c = 1\) in 1996 and \(c = 2.58\) in 2013 (Noufaily et al. 2013).

4. Keep the trend only if it is worth keeping. The \(\beta t\) term survives when \(\beta\) is significant at 5%, when the predicted \(\mu_0\) does not exceed the largest count in the reference set, and (in the R implementation) when \(b \ge 3\). Otherwise the whole procedure is repeated with an intercept-only model. A trend estimated from \(b\) clusters a year apart and then extrapolated a further year can blow \(\mu_0\) up exponentially, which is what the second condition guards against. In 2013 the significance test is dropped — with 200-odd points the slope is stable enough — but both safety catches remain.

5. Take the upper end of the prediction interval. Two things are uncertain: the count itself, with variance \(\varphi\mu_0\), and the estimated mean, with variance \(\operatorname{Var}(\mu_0)\). The 1996 threshold adds them on the \(2/3\) power scale, where a Poisson count is close to symmetric:

\[ U = \mu_0\left(1 + \frac{2}{3}\,z\,\sqrt{\frac{\varphi\mu_0 + \operatorname{Var}(\mu_0)}{\mu_0^{2}}}\,\right)^{3/2}. \]

The 2013 threshold instead reads a quantile straight off a negative binomial with the same mean and variance:

\[ U = F^{-1}_{\mathrm{NB}}\!\left(1-\alpha;\ \text{size} = \frac{\mu_0}{\varphi - 1},\ \text{prob} = \frac{1}{\varphi}\right). \]

6. Score it, and refuse to alarm on almost nothing. The result is reported as an exceedance score,

\[ X = \frac{y_0 - \mu_0}{U - \mu_0}, \]

which is dimensionless: \(X < 1\) is inside the interval, \(X = 1\) sits exactly on the threshold, \(X = 2\) is twice as far past expectation as it needed to be. A rare organism at 9 cases against 2 expected and a common one at 520 against 400 can be ranked on the same scale. Finally, a rule of thumb against alarms on near-empty series: no alarm unless there are at least 5 cases in the last 4 weeks.

4.2.1 Reading the threshold formula

The 1996 expression is easier to trust once unpacked. Work on the scale \(y^{2/3}\), where the right skew of a Poisson count largely disappears. On that scale the standard deviation of the prediction error is \(\tfrac{2}{3}\mu_0^{1/6}\sqrt{\tau}\), with

\[ \tau = \varphi + \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\mu_0)}{\mu_0}. \]

Add \(z\) of them to \(\mu_0^{2/3}\) and raise the result back to the power \(3/2\):

\[ U = \left(\mu_0^{2/3} + z\,\tfrac{2}{3}\,\mu_0^{1/6}\sqrt{\tau}\right)^{3/2}. \]

That is the same formula as above, and it is line for line what the package computes.

Ignoring the uncertainty in the mean altogether costs about a sixth of a case. Changing \(\varphi\) from 1 to 3 moves the threshold by five and a half. \(\varphi\) and \(z\) are what set the distance between expectation and threshold; the delta-method refinement is a rounding detail beside them.

For the same numbers the negative binomial rule gives \(U = 17\), against 17.13 from the \(2/3\) formula — the two threshold rules agree closely. They differ in kind rather than in value: the NB quantile is always a whole number and needs no back transformation, which is what matters on sparse series, but it plugs \(\mu_0\) and \(\varphi\) straight in and ignores their uncertainty.

4.3 The cut-off that decides how much history to believe

Step 3 is where the two versions actually part company, and the reason is a piece of arithmetic the 1996 authors did not have in front of them.

If nothing is wrong, the standardised residuals are roughly standard normal. So a cut-off of 1 catches about 16% of perfectly ordinary weeks and shrinks them. The weeks it shrinks hardest are the ones with the largest squared residuals — precisely the ones carrying the information about how much ordinary weeks scatter. The result is that \(\varphi\) is pulled down, often onto its own floor of 1, the threshold follows it down, and false alarms go up. Raising the cut-off to 2.58 leaves about 0.5% of ordinary weeks affected, so only something genuinely extreme is discounted.

The figure holds the data fixed and moves only the cut-off, and the three regimes come out clearly. Turn reweighting off and the old outbreak counts in full: \(\varphi\) stays at 2.05, the threshold rises to 16.3, and a new outbreak would have to be very large to clear it. Set the cut-off to 1 and three of the 35 weeks are shrunk, \(\varphi\) collapses onto its floor of 1.00, and the threshold drops to 12.89. Set it to 2.58 and only the single most extreme week is touched, \(\varphi\) settles at 1.28, and the threshold lands at 14.12.

The week being assessed notified 13 cases. Under the 1996 cut-off that is an alarm; under the 2013 cut-off it is not. Same data, same formula, same week — one parameter.

Two properties of the weighting rule are easy to miss. It is one-sided: a week that is unusually low keeps its full weight, which makes this a filter for past outbreaks rather than robust regression in general. And \(\gamma\) does not move the fitted line at all; it exists only to keep the weights averaging 1, so that the \(n - p\) denominator in step 2 still means something.

4.4 What the revision bought

An evaluation by the same group across 959 disease series puts numbers on the trade: the false alarm rate fell from 1.63% to 0.76% for the negative binomial version and 0.87% for the quasi-Poisson one, while the probability of detection fell from 57% to 51% and 53% respectively (Enki et al. 2016). Public Health England moved to the negative binomial version. Read the two rows together: roughly half the false alarms, at the cost of a few points of sensitivity — a less flattering picture than “without reducing sensitivity”, and the honest one to quote.

4.5 Running it

farringtonFlexible() in R’s surveillance package runs either version depending on the control list (Salmon et al. 2016). Three things about it cause trouble.

The defaults are neither version. They take the reference set, the trend test and the threshold formula from 1996, but the weight cut-off 2.58 from 2013. For the 2013 method you have to ask for it:

control <- list(range = weeks,
                b = 5, w = 3,
                noPeriods = 10,              # the seasonal factor
                pastWeeksNotIncluded = 26,
                pThresholdTrend = 1,         # always keep the trend
                weightsThreshold = 2.58,
                thresholdMethod = "nbPlugin",
                alpha = 0.05)                # one-sided here
res <- farringtonFlexible(sts_object, control)

alpha means different things in different functions. algo.farrington() uses qnorm(1 - alpha/2), two-sided; farringtonFlexible() uses the \(1-\alpha\) quantile, one-sided. At the same nominal alpha the newer function sets a lower threshold and raises more alarms, and comparing the two means doubling alpha for one of them.

Misspelled names in control are ignored in silence. There is no error and no warning; the run simply uses the default. Check the returned object rather than assuming the setting took.

Two more things worth knowing before building a pipeline on it. The earliest week that can be assessed is \(52b + w + 1\), so five years of history buys you nothing until week 264. And weeks failing the 5-in-4 rule come back with upperbound = NA, which breaks the threshold line into pieces on sparse series — decide in advance whether to relax the rule or aggregate the units.

4.6 What an alarm is, and is not

A Farrington alarm says one thing: this week’s count is higher than a model fitted to comparable weeks in the past predicted, at the chosen error rate. ECDC’s own documentation for the method states plainly that such signals “do not necessarily correspond to outbreaks”, and are there to triage many series efficiently (European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, n.d.).

Every agency running it pairs it with filters and with people. UKHSA sets \(\alpha = 0.005\). France’s food-chain Salmonella system keeps only signals that three algorithms agree on, or that persist beyond two weeks. The reason is arithmetic rather than caution: at a one-sided \(\alpha = 0.05\) with a correct model, each series crosses its threshold by chance about one week in twenty, so 168 wards monitored at that level produce roughly eight chance signals every week. That is the same multiple-comparisons problem as Section 3.4, and it does not care which algorithm raised the signal.

Three structural limits are worth stating alongside the method, because they follow from its shape rather than from any implementation.

It judges each week alone. There is no accumulation of evidence, so a rise that is small but sustained can pass without any single week clearing the threshold. That is exactly the gap CUSUM fills, and the package’s own documentation suggests pairing the two.

It assumes the season has the same shape every year. The seasonal factor is estimated from past years and applied to this one, so a season arriving early or late is compared against the wrong step.

It has nowhere to put a covariate. Temperature, rainfall and population movement cannot enter the model, which is the limitation the Bayesian chapter addresses directly.

One more thing it does not do, which is easy to expect of it. A normal seasonal rise is not an alarm. The method answers “is this week unusual for this time of year”, not “has the season started”. For the second question you need a different tool.
