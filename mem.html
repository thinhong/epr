5  Moving Epidemic Method

The Moving Epidemic Method (MEM) sets thresholds for weekly influenza-like illness (ILI) or acute respiratory infection (ARI) rates from past seasons (Vega et al. 2013). This chapter follows its steps one by one, with a figure for each step. The data are weekly ILI consultation rates per 100,000 in Croatia, week 26 of 2022 to week 43 of 2026, from ECDC’s European Respiratory Virus Surveillance Summary (ERVISS) (European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control 2026). In each figure, point at a blue symbol in a formula to see where it is in the chart.

5.1 The method in brief

MEM uses past seasons to set thresholds for the next season. It works in three steps.

  1. Find the epidemic weeks. For each past season, the MAP curve gives the length and the timing of the epidemic. This splits the season into three periods: pre-epidemic, epidemic and post-epidemic.
  2. Set the epidemic threshold. From each pre-epidemic period, take the \(n\) highest weekly rates, where \(n = 30 / \text{number of seasons}\). Pool these values from all seasons. The epidemic threshold is their mean plus \(z_{0.05}\) standard deviations, where \(z_{0.05} = 1.645\). A post-epidemic threshold comes from the post-epidemic periods in the same way.
  3. Set the intensity thresholds. From each epidemic period, take the \(n\) highest weekly rates and pool them. Their geometric mean and upper limits give the thresholds for the levels of intensity.

In a new season, the first weekly rate above the epidemic threshold signals the start of the epidemic. The intensity thresholds then give the level of each week.

The purpose of the MEM is to model influenza epidemics based upon historical data from a specific country or region. The method has three main steps: First, the length, start and the end of the annual epidemics are determined, splitting the season in three periods: a pre-epidemic, an epidemic and a post-epidemic period. Secondly, the baseline and the epidemic thresholds are calculated using the pre-epidemic and post-epidemic values of historical seasons. Finally, thresholds for different levels of intensity are computed. (Vega et al. 2013)

5.2 The data: one row per season

Weekly rates were estimated at least for 33 weeks in every season, from week 40 of 1 year to the week 20 of the following year. (Vega et al. 2013)

Missing values in weekly rates at the beginning or at the end of the surveillance period did not affect the method and were excluded from calculations. Missing weekly data during the surveillance period were imputed with a smooth regression over the full season’s data using a small window parameter. (Vega et al. 2013)

A real series needs this preparation. Some weeks were never reported, some weeks fall between seasons, and the current season is not finished. The paper used countries with five or more seasons of data (Vega et al. 2013). ERVISS has four complete seasons for Croatia, so the numbers in this chapter are a worked example, not Croatia’s official thresholds.

5.3 Step 1: the epidemic period of each season

In the first step, for each season separately, the length of the epidemic period is estimated as the minimum number of consecutive weeks with the maximum accumulated rates percentage (MAP). (Vega et al. 2013)

The MAP curve draws the maximum cumulated rate for a period of a given length \(r\), expressed as percentage of the total rate of the \(j\)th season. (Vega et al. 2013)

Then the MAP curve is smoothed, using a smoothing regression (over \(r\)), selecting automatically the window parameter. The optimum number of weeks for each season is determined calculating the increment in the percentage from one number of weeks to the next one. … When this increment is lower than the predefined criteria, the optimum is found. (Vega et al. 2013)

The criterion is \(\delta\). It depends on the country, ranges from 2% to 4% and is chosen to maximise sensitivity and specificity (Vega et al. 2013). Once the optimum number of weeks \(r^*\) is found, the epidemic starts at week \(k^*\), the first week of the window of \(r^*\) weeks with the largest total. The weeks before the epidemic are the pre-epidemic period, and the weeks after it are the post-epidemic period (Vega et al. 2013).

5.4 Step 2: the epidemic threshold

In the second step, the baseline and threshold using pre-epidemic values of historical seasons are calculated. The baseline is the arithmetic mean of all pre-epidemic rates of all historical seasons (pooled together as a single sample). (Vega et al. 2013)

To calculate the threshold, only a set of pre-epidemic values is considered. For each season, the highest \(n\) values from the pre-epidemic period are taken, where \(n\) = 30/number of seasons. (Vega et al. 2013)

With all these \(n \times N\) values, a one-tailed point 95% confidence interval is calculated. … The upper limit of the confidence interval is the epidemic threshold. Additionally, a post-epidemic threshold is calculated using the same procedure. (Vega et al. 2013)

So the epidemic threshold is \(t^* = \bar{t} + z_{0.05} \times S_t\), where \(\bar{t}\) and \(S_t\) are the mean and the standard deviation of the \(n \times N\) values, and \(z_{0.05} = 1.645\). With four seasons, \(n = 30 / 4 = 7.5\), which the authors’ R package rounds to 8 (Lozano 2025), so 32 values are pooled.

NoteWhat is \(z_{0.05}\)?

\(z_{0.05}\) is the point of the standard normal curve with 5% of the area above it. Its value is 1.645.

The calculation assumes that the pooled values come from a normal distribution with their mean and standard deviation. In any normal distribution, 95% of the values lie below the mean plus \(z_{0.05}\) standard deviations, and 5% lie above it.

The interval is one-tailed because only high rates matter for an alarm, so all of the 5% is in the upper tail. A two-tailed 95% interval would put 2.5% in each tail and use \(z_{0.025} = 1.96\) instead.

The interval is a point interval: it is for one weekly value, not for the mean. So it uses the standard deviation, not the standard error. In plain words, if the highest pre-epidemic weeks of a new season follow the same distribution as in past seasons, about 1 in 20 of them would still be above the threshold.

5.5 Step 3: the intensity thresholds

Thresholds for different levels of intensity are calculated using the \(n\) highest values of each epidemic period to build a sample of \(n \times N\) rates. With the sample, the geometric mean and 50%, 90% and 95% one-sided confidence intervals are calculated. These upper limits are the threshold for the different levels of intensity of the epidemic. (Vega et al. 2013)

As in step 2, \(z_\alpha\) is the point of the standard normal curve with \(\alpha\) of the area above it. Step 3 uses it on the logarithms of the rates: \(z_{0.50} = 0\) for the 50% limit, which is the geometric mean itself, \(z_{0.10} = 1.282\) for the 90% limit and \(z_{0.05} = 1.645\) for the 95% limit. The three thresholds split the weekly rates into five levels: baseline, low, medium, high and very high (Vega et al. 2015). The paper says countries may change these percentages (Vega et al. 2013). Vega et al. (2015) and the authors’ R package (Lozano 2025) use 40%, 90% and 97.5%.

5.6 Step 4: watching a new season

The main purpose of the method is to define the baseline influenza activity in historical data and to establish an epidemic threshold above which the weekly rates are considered to be in the epidemic period. (Vega et al. 2013)

Rates in the pre-epidemic period were expected to be lower than the pre-epidemic threshold rate, while rates within the epidemic period are expected to be higher. Finally, rates in the post-epidemic period were expected to be lower than the post-epidemic threshold rate. (Vega et al. 2013)

The paper calls the first week with a rate above the epidemic threshold the alert week (Vega et al. 2013). During the epidemic, the intensity thresholds give the level of each week. The paper gives no rule for the end of the epidemic in a new season; the authors’ R package ends it at the first week after the peak with a rate below the post-epidemic threshold (Lozano 2025).

If influenza weekly epidemiological data are accompanied by timely virological data, virus circulation should be taken into account to make appropriate public health decisions. (Vega et al. 2013)

Figure 5.6 starts with the current season, 2026/27, and the thresholds from all four past seasons. The data end in week 43 of 2026. To show how a season reaches the threshold, the weeks after that are illustrative, not data: each one is the average rate of the four past seasons in the same week.

For each country, each single season was extracted from the historical records to be used as the target season. MEM calculated the beginning and the end of the epidemic period of the target season. On the basis of the remaining seasons, excluding the target season, MEM calculated the pre- and post-epidemic thresholds. (Vega et al. 2013)
