2  RKI

The RKI method asks a narrower question than a control chart does. Not “is this week high?”, but “is this week high for this week of the year?” It gathers a handful of comparable weeks — the same week in earlier years, and the weeks just gone — and treats what happened in them as what should happen now. It is the instinct behind judging a June electricity bill against last June rather than against the yearly average. Set the three parameters below and watch which weeks get pulled into the comparison.

The Robert Koch Institute built this into SurvNet, Germany’s national electronic notification system, in 2001 (Faensen et al. 2006; Robert Koch Institute, n.d.). It follows the CDC method of Stroup and colleagues (Stroup et al. 1989), which introduced the idea of judging a week against the equivalent weeks of earlier years rather than against one overall average. Höhle’s surveillance package implements it as algo.rki (Höhle 2007; Höhle and Riebler, n.d.).

2.1 Computing an epidemic threshold with RKI

For weekly case counts the tool is algo.rki in R’s surveillance package, with algo.rki1, algo.rki2 and algo.rki3 as named presets (Salmon et al. 2016). It runs in three steps.

1. Reference set. Collect the weeks that count as comparable to week \(t\). They come from two places: a window of width \(2w+1\) centred on the equivalent week in each of the \(b\) previous years, and the \(w_0\) weeks immediately before \(t\) in the current year.

\[ R_{w,w_0,b} \;=\; \left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{b}\ \bigcup_{j=-w}^{w} y_{\,t-52i+j}\right) \;\cup\; \left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{w_0} y_{\,t-k}\right) \]

Here \(y_t\) is the number of cases notified in week \(t\), and 52 is the number of weeks the package assumes in a year.

2. Expected level. Average them.

\[ \hat\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{y \,\in\, R_{w,w_0,b}} y, \qquad n = \bigl|R_{w,w_0,b}\bigr|. \]

There is no fitted curve and no trend term. The reference weeks are the model.

3. Threshold. Take the upper limit of a two-sided 95% interval for a count of that size. algo.rki picks the interval two different ways, depending on how large \(\hat\mu\) is:

\[ U_t \;=\; \begin{cases} \hat\mu + 2\hat\sigma, & \hat\mu > 20,\\[4pt] \tfrac{1}{2}\,\chi^2_{0.975}\bigl(2(\lfloor\hat\mu\rfloor+1)\bigr), & \hat\mu \le 20, \end{cases} \]

with \(\hat\sigma\) the sample standard deviation of the reference values. A week signals when \(N_t > U_t\).

The defaults are \(b=2\), \(w=4\) and actY = FALSE — the RKI 3 system — the comparison is strict, so a week that exactly meets the threshold stays quiet, and \(U_t\) is never rounded to a whole case, so 24 notifications against a threshold of 23.49 is a signal.

2.1.1 How many weeks the comparison uses

The two sources of reference weeks never overlap, provided \(w_0 < 52 - w\), so the size of the set is just the two pieces added together. Count each one.

The current year contributes a single week for each \(k\) from 1 to \(w_0\):

\[ n_{\text{current}} = w_0. \]

Each past year contributes a single week for each \(j\) from \(-w\) to \(w\), which is \(2w+1\) weeks, and there are \(b\) such years:

\[ n_{\text{past}} = b\,(2w+1). \]

Add them:

\[ n = w_0 + b\,(2w+1). \]

Nothing here depends on the case counts. The size of the comparison is fixed by the calendar alone, decided before any data is read, which is what makes the three published systems directly comparable.

One implementation detail matters when you go to run it. In algo.rki the two widths are not independent: the current-year window is switched on by actY and reuses the same \(w\), so

\[ w_0 = w \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\texttt{actY}\}, \]

and the package labels its output rki(w, w0, b) accordingly. A setting like \(w=1\) with \(w_0=2\) is expressible in the definition above but not through algo.rki; it needs a hand-rolled reference set.

2.1.2 Where the two branches come from

Both branches answer one question: how large can a count get before it stops looking like a draw from the reference level?

When the expected count is large, the reference values are roughly symmetric about their mean, and the upper limit of a two-sided 95% interval sits about two standard deviations above it. The package rounds 1.96 to 2 and takes

\[ U_t = \hat\mu + 2\hat\sigma. \]

When the expected count is small that approximation fails: counts are discrete, the distribution is skewed, and a symmetric interval can reach below zero. So the package uses the exact Poisson limit instead. For an observation \(x\), the upper limit is the rate at which \(x\) or fewer cases would be seen only 2.5% of the time:

\[ \Pr\bigl(X \le x \mid \lambda = U\bigr) = 0.025, \]

which has a closed form in terms of the chi-square quantile,

\[ U = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\chi^2_{0.975}\bigl(2(x+1)\bigr). \]

algo.rki reads this out of a stored table (CIdata, tabulated by Sachs (Sachs 1991)) at \(x = \lfloor\hat\mu\rfloor\), the reference mean rounded down to a whole case. That floor is why \(U_t\) is a step function: it holds still while the reference mean drifts, then jumps the moment the mean crosses an integer.

A quicker approximation is sometimes quoted for this step, \(\hat\mu + 1.96\sqrt{\hat\mu}\), which treats the reference mean as a Poisson rate. It lands close, but it is not what the package computes, and it is the more permissive of the two — on the example below it gives 23.16 where the table gives 24.74.

2.1.3 The example in Figure 2.1

The figure opens on a worked case. Week 25 of 2026 is assessed with \(b=3\), \(w=1\) and \(w_0=2\), so the reference set is the three weeks around week 25 in each of 2025, 2024 and 2023, plus weeks 23 and 24 of 2026 — eleven weeks in all, from the formula \(n = 2 + 3(2\cdot 1 + 1) = 11\). They average 15.45 cases. That is below 20, so the Poisson branch applies: \(\lfloor 15.45 \rfloor = 15\), and the table gives \(U = 24.74\). Twenty-one notifications would pass; twenty-six would signal.

Raising \(w_0\) pulls more of the current year into the comparison, and lowering it pushes the method back onto earlier years. That single trade is what separates the three published systems.

2.2 RKI 1, RKI 2 and RKI 3

RKI 1 — \(b=0\), \(w=6\), actY = TRUE, six reference weeks. The six weeks just gone, and nothing else. It has no memory of previous years, so it cannot know what time of year it is; what it can do is react within six weeks of a change.

RKI 2 — \(b=1\), \(w=6\), actY = TRUE, nineteen reference weeks. The same six recent weeks, plus the thirteen weeks centred on the equivalent week one year ago. Roughly half recent, half seasonal.

RKI 3 — \(b=2\), \(w=4\), actY = FALSE, eighteen reference weeks. The current year is dropped entirely; nine weeks are taken around the equivalent week in each of the two previous years. Every reference week is seasonal.

The consequence is visible in Figure 2.2. Because RKI 1 and RKI 2 include the weeks immediately before \(t\), a rise that lasts more than a few weeks enters its own reference set and lifts its own threshold behind it. The simulated rise in the fourth year of the figure spans weeks 14 to 25 of 2025: RKI 1 flags two of those weeks, RKI 2 flags four, and RKI 3 — which never looks at the current year — flags six.

That is not a ranking. It is a trade, and it runs in both directions. RKI 3 keeps a baseline no ongoing outbreak can inflate, but it cannot report at all until two full years of history exist behind it; in the figure it stays silent until week 5 of 2024, while RKI 1 is running by week 7 of 2022. A short memory buys speed and a short warm-up and pays for it in seasonal blindness. A long one buys a seasonal baseline and pays for it in start-up time and in slower recognition that the baseline itself has shifted.

The series in Figure 2.2 is simulated, not observed. It is there to show how the threshold behaves, not how dengue behaves.

2.3 Poisson or negative binomial

Every threshold so far has assumed that a week’s count varies about as much as its own average. That is what lets \(\sqrt{\hat\mu}\) carry the whole of step 3: Poisson counts have variance equal to their mean, so the mean alone fixes the spread, and no separate variance has to be estimated.

The choice between the two distributions is not a choice about which disease you are watching. It comes down to one question about the reference weeks: do they vary more than their own mean?

Poisson says no. It holds when cases arrive independently, at a rate that does not move across the reference weeks. Three things break that, and any one is enough:

  • Cases are not independent. One case produces others, so they arrive in lumps rather than one at a time.
  • The rate itself moves. Whatever drives transmission changes across the reference weeks, so they are not draws from a single distribution.
  • Reporting is uneven. A delayed week followed by a double week adds variance that has nothing to do with transmission.

All three inflate the variance and leave the mean alone — the one failure Poisson cannot express, because it has no parameter for it.

2.3.1 What it costs to get this wrong

Write the excess as a dispersion factor \(\varphi = \operatorname{Var}(y) / \operatorname{E}(y)\), so \(\varphi = 1\) under Poisson. A negative binomial with mean \(\mu\) and dispersion \(\varphi\) has variance \(\varphi\mu\), so its standard deviation is

\[ \sqrt{\varphi\mu} \;=\; \sqrt{\varphi}\,\sqrt{\mu}. \]

The distance from the mean up to any quantile is a multiple of the standard deviation, so it widens by \(\sqrt{\varphi}\), not by \(\varphi\). Doubling the variance moves the threshold out by about 41%.

Small in the threshold, large in the alarm rate. The algo.rki Poisson branch starts out conservative — a confidence limit read from a table, not a predictive quantile — so on true Poisson counts it fires on about one normal week in a hundred, not the one in twenty its 95% label suggests. Hold that threshold fixed and let the counts be overdispersed:

At \(\varphi = 3\), a threshold meant to fire on about 1% of normal weeks fires on 9%: a false alarm every eleven weeks in place of one every two years. In cases, the worked example’s threshold of 24.74 would have to sit at 33.

The error runs one way only. Assuming Poisson when \(\varphi > 1\) costs specificity, never sensitivity.

2.3.2 Choosing

\(\varphi\) is estimated, not assumed. Estimate it from a long stretch of non-epidemic weeks — not from the reference set, whose 18 or 19 values are far too few. The usual estimator is the Pearson dispersion of a quasi-Poisson fit with the seasonality left in:

fit <- glm(cases ~ t + sin(2*pi*week/52) + cos(2*pi*week/52),
           family = quasipoisson, data = baseline)
summary(fit)$dispersion

Near 1, stay with Poisson. algo.rki works as it stands, needs no second estimate, and a badly fitted dispersion is its own source of error.

Above about 2, move to the negative binomial. Table 2.1 applies, and the Poisson threshold is delivering several times the alarm rate it claims.

In between, prefer Poisson when the baseline is short. A \(\hat\varphi\) drawn from two years of weekly data is itself uncertain, and over-estimating it raises the threshold and hides outbreaks Poisson would have caught.

algo.rki has no negative binomial option. The smallest change keeps its reference set and replaces only the last step: a negative binomial with mean \(\hat\mu\) and variance \(\hat\varphi\hat\mu\) has size parameter \(k = \hat\mu / (\hat\varphi - 1)\), so

U <- qnbinom(1 - alpha, mu = mu_hat, size = mu_hat / (phi_hat - 1))

The reference weeks, the calendar and the decision rule are unchanged; only the tail is. Beyond that, farringtonFlexible estimates dispersion as part of fitting and downweights past outbreaks so they cannot inflate the baseline (Farrington et al. 1996; Noufaily et al. 2013), and boda fits a negative binomial outright (Salmon et al. 2016) — both at far more computation than RKI.
