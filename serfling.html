6  Serfling

6.1 Overview

Every method so far has asked whether this week is unusual for this time of year. The Farrington chapter ended on a question that kind of method cannot answer — has the epidemic season started? — because a normal seasonal rise is, by construction, not unusual for its time of year. Serfling’s method models what the year would look like with no epidemic in it, and treats anything well above that line as epidemic activity. Like a tide table, which predicts the water level from a few regular cycles so that a storm surge is whatever it did not predict, Serfling’s baseline is a slow trend plus a few waves of fixed length, fitted to years with the epidemics taken out.

Robert E. Serfling published the method in 1963 at the US Communicable Disease Center (CDC), to monitor weekly deaths from pneumonia and influenza (P&I) in 108 US cities (Serfling 1963). He chose P&I deaths although they lag influenza cases by about four weeks, because case reports and school or work absences were not quantitative enough or not available widely. A parametric periodic regression model, it remains sixty years on one of the foundations of epidemic thresholds (Unkel et al. 2012).

For a seasonal disease one fixed threshold does not work: low enough to catch an epidemic outside the season, it fires all through the peak season; high enough for the peak season, it misses epidemics outside it. Serfling lets the threshold move with the seasonal baseline.

NoteCore idea

Epidemic threshold = the expected level with no epidemic + a safety margin for random variation. The variants of Serfling differ mainly in how they estimate these two components.

6.2 The procedure at a glance

The method rests on three ideas: a baseline built from a long-term trend and sine and cosine waves (a Fourier series); training data cleaned of past epidemics, so that they do not pull the baseline up; and a threshold a margin above the baseline, usually 1.64–1.645 standard deviations of the error, that must be crossed two weeks in a row (Serfling 1963). In practice that is four steps (Figure 6.1). Section 6.3 builds the model behind step 2; Section 6.4 details each step and works through all four on one data set.

6.3 Mathematical basis

6.3.1 The general equation

The Serfling model with \(r\) harmonics has the form (Serfling 1963):

\[ E\left(Y_t\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \sum_{i=1}^{r}\left[\gamma_i\cos\left(\frac{2\pi i t}{L}\right) + \delta_i\sin\left(\frac{2\pi i t}{L}\right)\right] \tag{6.1}\]

Each parameter has one job (Figure 6.2): \(\beta_0\) sets the level, \(\beta_1\) tilts the curve from year to year, and each pair \(\gamma_i,\ \delta_i\) sets how tall one seasonal wave is and when it peaks. The trend can also be quadratic or cubic when the data change nonlinearly (Pelat et al. 2007). Serfling wrote the equation as \(\hat{Y} = u + bt + \sum a_i\cos\theta + \sum b_i\sin\theta\), with \(\theta\) linear in \(t\) (Serfling 1963); this lesson uses \(\beta\), \(\gamma\) and \(\delta\) to avoid clashing with later notation.

6.3.2 Fourier series, intuitively

Any curve that repeats every year can be assembled from sine and cosine waves of different frequencies, as a chord is built from single notes. Harmonic 1 has period \(L\) (52 weeks) and rises and falls once a year; harmonic 2 has period \(L/2\) (26 weeks) and rises and falls twice; harmonic \(i\) rises and falls \(i\) times. At each harmonic, the cosine–sine pair is one wave with an amplitude and a phase:

\[ \gamma_i\cos\left(\frac{2\pi i t}{L}\right) + \delta_i\sin\left(\frac{2\pi i t}{L}\right) = A_i\cos\left(\frac{2\pi i t}{L} - \theta_i\right),\qquad A_i = \sqrt{\gamma_i^2 + \delta_i^2} \]

\(A_i\) is how tall the wave is and \(\theta_i\) when it peaks. The cosine alone peaks at the start of the cycle, the sine alone a quarter of a cycle later; mixing them in the ratio \(\gamma_i : \delta_i\) puts the peak in any week. And because \(\gamma_i\) and \(\delta_i\) enter the model linearly, ordinary linear regression estimates them; no nonlinear fit of the phase is needed. The analyst chooses only \(L\) and \(r\): the frequencies are fixed, and the data decide how tall each wave is and where it peaks.

Figure 6.3 lets you do the regression’s job by hand: set the four coefficients until your curve runs through a year of weekly data, then compare with least squares, which makes the squared distances as small as possible.

The first-order model (\(r = 1\)) gives exactly one symmetric peak a year:

\[ Y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \gamma_1\cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{52}\right) + \delta_1\sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{52}\right) + \varepsilon_t \]

The second-order model (\(r = 2\)) adds a semi-annual wave, allowing two peaks a year or one asymmetric peak that rises fast and falls slowly:

\[ Y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \gamma_1\cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{52}\right) + \delta_1\sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{52}\right) + \gamma_2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{26}\right) + \delta_2\sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{26}\right) + \varepsilon_t \]

A larger \(r\) follows more complex shapes, but pushed too far the model fits random noise and leftover epidemic peaks as well. So \(r\) is chosen by a statistical test, not by eye.

6.3.3 Choosing the number of harmonics: the example in the original paper

Serfling fitted the model to P&I deaths in 108 US cities over three seasons without a major epidemic (leaving out the 1957–58 pandemic), in thirteen four-week periods a year (Serfling 1963). To decide on a second harmonic he used Bliss’s analysis of variance, a partial F test: the variance added by harmonic 2 (mean square 8,136) was smaller than the residual variance of the second-order model (13,157), so \(F \approx 0.62 < 1\). Harmonic 2 explained no more than noise, and the first-order model was kept:

\[ \hat{Y}_t = 300.5 + 2.1\, t + 97.6\cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{13} - 2.67\right) \tag{6.2}\]

Here \(t\) is in four-week periods, from the midpoint of the period ending 24 September 1955. In this amplitude–phase form the amplitude is 97.6 deaths per week and the peak falls at \(t = 2.67 \times 13/2\pi \approx 5.5\), around February 1956 (Figure 6.4). In R the same test is anova(m1, m2); models can also be compared by AIC (Pelat et al. 2007).

6.4 Computing an epidemic threshold with Serfling

The four steps of Figure 6.1, in detail. Throughout, the aim is to separate ordinary baseline variation from what past epidemics left behind.

1. Clean the history. Remove or downweight past epidemics before fitting, so the baseline describes a year without them. Five ways are in use:

  • By calendar. Drop seasons or weeks known to be epidemic. Serfling used only non-epidemic seasons, replaced isolated high weeks with the trend value when estimating the spread, and, when many years in a row had epidemics, estimated the trend from the low season alone, roughly mid-May to mid-September (Serfling 1963). EuroMOMO fits only spring and autumn weeks, least affected by winter influenza and summer heat (EuroMOMO, n.d.).
  • By percentile. Drop a fixed share of the highest training weeks, typically about 15% (Pelat et al. 2007, 2017); on French influenza-like illness data the best configuration cut at the 86th percentile and used the full history (Souty et al. 2018).
  • Iteratively. Fit, drop the weeks above the fitted line and refit; then drop weeks above the upper 95% prediction limit and refit, until \(R^2\) stops increasing (Wang et al. 2015).
  • Robustly. Keep every week but fit by iteratively reweighted estimation, such as MASS::rlm, so unusual weeks lose weight on their own (Pelat et al. 2017). The CDC’s P&I baseline used a robust procedure (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 1998).
  • As censored. Treat each epidemic week as left-censored — without the epidemic it would have been at most what was observed — and mark weeks as epidemic one at a time, largest positive residual first, up to a set share (about 16% for acute respiratory infection) (Procházka and Kynčl 2016).

2. Fit and extrapolate. Estimate the coefficients on the cleaned data (by least squares, or a weighted or likelihood variant) and project \(\hat Y_t\) over the weeks to be monitored. That projection is the seasonal baseline, what to expect with no epidemic. Five years of training is common (EuroMOMO, n.d.; Pelat et al. 2017) but worth testing: on the French data the full history beat windows of 3, 5 or 10 years (Souty et al. 2018).

3. Set the threshold. Even with no epidemic the series scatters around the baseline, so the threshold sits a multiple of the forecast error above it:

\[ \text{Threshold}_t = \hat Y_t + z_\alpha\,\hat\sigma_t, \]

where \(\hat\sigma_t\), the standard deviation of the forecast error, combines the residual spread with the uncertainty of extrapolating. Serfling and the CDC use \(z = 1.64\)–\(1.645\) (Serfling 1963; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 1998); Serfling corrected for the extrapolated trend but not for the Fourier terms. This is the upper end of a one-sided 95% (or two-sided 90%) prediction interval, level = 0.90 in R’s predict() (McConeghy, n.d.). Santé publique France uses \(z \approx 1.96\), trading sensitivity for specificity (Pelat et al. 2017). On a log-normal model the threshold is \(\exp(\hat\mu_t + z_\alpha\hat\sigma)\), computed on the log scale (Procházka and Kynčl 2016).

4. Require two weeks. A single high week is as often a reporting delay or a data error as an epidemic. The onset is declared only when the series stays above the threshold two weeks in a row (Serfling 1963; Wang et al. 2015).

6.4.1 Working through the four steps

Step 1 decides the quality of the whole method: fit raw history and every past epidemic pulls the baseline up and swells the residual spread, so the threshold lands too high and new epidemics are flagged late (Unkel et al. 2012). Figure 6.5 simulates five training seasons, each with a winter epidemic, and a sixth to monitor. Because the data are simulated, the true baseline is known, and so is the real false alarm rate of any threshold. Choose a cleaning method, step through, then switch methods at any step to compare.

Untrimmed, the threshold barely fires and the epidemic is confirmed one week before its peak. Each cleaning method succeeds or fails for its own reason:

  • Calendar does best here because every epidemic falls inside the window (weeks 14–40, roughly October to March); it fails when one does not, and it leaves fewer weeks to fit.
  • Percentile is only as good as its share, which should be roughly the share of weeks actually epidemic: a quarter here, while the common 15% was tuned on French influenza-like illness (Souty et al. 2018). Trim 40% and the normal seasonal peak goes too; an ordinary week then crosses the threshold almost one time in five.
  • Iterative cleaning drops every week above the first fitted line, here 75 ordinary weeks with 45 epidemic ones, so the baseline ends about 10 too low in summer and 10 too high in winter, and \(s\) underestimates the noise.
  • Robust regression only reduces the weight of large residuals; with a quarter of the weeks epidemic, they still pull the baseline up. On the French data, too, it was less sensitive than periodic regression with trimming (Souty et al. 2018).

Cleaning also decides \(r\): untrimmed, the test for a second harmonic gives \(F = 27.8\), because the extra wave chases the epidemic peaks; at 15% trimming it falls to 1.6 (step 2 of the figure).

6.4.2 Why two weeks in a row

With \(z = 1.645\) an ordinary week still crosses the threshold about 5% of the time, two to three weeks a year (Serfling 1963). Over a 26-week winter–spring with no epidemic and independent weeks, the chance that at least one crosses is \(1 - 0.95^{26} = 0.736\). Two weeks in a row leaves 25 adjacent pairs, each crossing with probability \(0.05^2\), so the chance falls to at most \(25 \times 0.0025 = 0.0625\) — exactly 0.058; Serfling quoted about one in ten.

The price is time. The chance that a genuine rise is still unflagged after its second week is 0.9 for a moderate epidemic and 0.7 for a large one; after the third, 0.1 and under 0.01 (Serfling 1963). The arithmetic also assumes independent weeks; Serfling noted they are slightly correlated, and at a correlation of 0.4 the two-week false alarm rate triples, to about 17.5% (Figure 6.6).

6.4.3 Excess burden

Once the epidemic period is identified, the model also estimates the excess burden, in one of two ways:

  • Against the baseline: \(\text{Excess}_t = Y_t - \hat{Y}_t\), summed over the epidemic weeks (Serfling 1963).
  • Against the threshold (more conservative): \(\text{Excess}_t = \max\left(Y_t - \text{Threshold}_t,\ 0\right)\), counting only the part above the threshold (McConeghy, n.d.).

6.5 Variants and practical implementation

6.5.1 P&I mortality surveillance at the US CDC

The CDC applied Serfling to the percentage of all deaths due to pneumonia and influenza; a percentage is less biased than a count when the number of reporting units changes from week to week. The baseline came from periodic regression with a robust procedure, and the threshold was 1.645 standard deviations above it (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 1998). In October 2016 the data source moved from the 122 Cities Mortality Reporting System to the mortality surveillance system of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2016).

6.5.2 Serfling–Poisson and EuroMOMO

Least squares assumes normal residuals with constant variance: reasonable for large counts, poor for small or sparse ones, or counts split by age group and small area (Unkel et al. 2012). The remedy is a generalised linear model (GLM) with a log link and a Poisson distribution corrected for overdispersion (quasi-Poisson):

\[ \log\left(\mu_t\right) = \log\left(N_t\right) + \eta_0 + \eta_1 t + \eta_2\sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{52}\right) + \eta_3\cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{52}\right) \]

where \(\mu_t\) is the expected count and \(\log\left(N_t\right)\) a population offset, included when population data exist so that the model estimates a rate.

This is the structure of EuroMOMO (European monitoring of excess mortality): a quasi-Poisson GLM with a linear trend and an annual sine wave, fitted on the spring and autumn weeks of up to five previous years, with a correction for delays in death registration (EuroMOMO, n.d.). FluMOMO adds influenza activity and extreme temperatures as explanatory variables (Nielsen et al. 2018); the modulation model lets the trend and the seasonal amplitude change smoothly over time through P-splines (Leger et al. 2025).

6.5.3 Implementation in R

WarningA note on using fluserf

In the current source code, y0_ul is computed as y0 + 1.645 × se.fit, the standard error of the fitted line rather than of the forecast. The threshold is therefore narrower than the CDC’s definition. Check it before using it in operation.

A minimal example of the Serfling OLS model with percentile trimming and the two-week rule:

# df: weekly data with a column y (cases); the first 260 weeks are used for training
df$t  <- seq_len(nrow(df))
train <- df[df$t <= 260, ]
clean <- train[train$y <= quantile(train$y, 0.85), ]   # drop the highest 15% of weeks

m1 <- lm(y ~ t + cos(2*pi*t/52) + sin(2*pi*t/52), data = clean)
m2 <- update(m1, . ~ . + cos(4*pi*t/52) + sin(4*pi*t/52))
anova(m1, m2)                                           # is harmonic 2 needed?

new  <- df[df$t > 260, ]
pred <- predict(m1, newdata = new, interval = "prediction", level = 0.90)
new$baseline  <- pred[, "fit"]
new$threshold <- pred[, "upr"]                          # baseline + 1.645 x forecast error
over          <- new$y > new$threshold
new$alarm     <- over & c(FALSE, head(over, -1))        # above the threshold 2 weeks running

6.6 Comparison with other methods

Serfling works like a Shewhart control chart: each week is compared with the threshold on its own. That catches sudden surges well but is less sensitive to slow, smouldering rises (Unkel et al. 2012). This section compares it with three commonly used groups of methods.

6.6.1 The Moving Epidemic Method (MEM)

MEM, developed by Vega and colleagues for influenza surveillance in Europe (Vega et al. 2013), does not fit one wave to the whole year. It splits each past season into pre-epidemic, epidemic and post-epidemic periods using the maximum accumulated rate percentage (MAP) curve, and sets the threshold for a new season at the upper limit of a one-sided confidence interval of the highest pre-epidemic values. On influenza-like illness data from the French Sentinelles network, against an expert-consensus gold standard, Souty and colleagues found (Souty et al. 2018) (Figure 6.7):

  • Serfling (with percentile trimming) and MEM detected the epidemic start week equally well.
  • Serfling had higher specificity and positive predictive value; MEM had higher sensitivity.
  • Robust regression detected onsets less well and was the only method to split one epidemic season into two waves.

6.6.2 The Farrington algorithm

The Farrington algorithm (Farrington et al. 1996) and Noufaily’s improved version (Noufaily et al. 2013) are widely used in European routine surveillance and available in R’s surveillance package (Salmon et al. 2016). Farrington handles seasonality through its reference window: for week \(t_0\) it takes only the weeks within \(\pm w\) of the same time in the \(b\) previous years (for example \(w = 3\), \(b = 5\)) and fits a log-linear quasi-Poisson model:

\[ \log\left(\mu_t\right) = \theta_0 + \theta_1 t,\qquad Var\left(y_t\right) = \phi\,\mu_t \]

Past epidemics are downweighted by their residuals, and the threshold is computed after a 2/3 power transformation that makes even small counts close to normal:

\[ U_{t_0} = \hat{\mu}_{t_0}\left\{1 + \frac{2}{3}\, z_\alpha\,\frac{\left[\hat{\phi}\,\hat{\mu}_{t_0} + var\left(\hat{\mu}_{t_0}\right)\right]^{1/2}}{\hat{\mu}_{t_0}}\right\}^{3/2} \]

With its quasi-Poisson structure and local window, Farrington suits low or sparse counts better than classical Serfling. It does not account for serial autocorrelation (Unkel et al. 2012).

6.6.3 The cumulative sum control chart (CUSUM)

CUSUM accumulates the positive standardised deviations over consecutive weeks:

\[ z_t = \frac{y_t - \mu_t}{\sigma_t},\qquad C_t = \max\left(0,\ C_{t-1} + z_t - k\right) \]

and raises an alarm when \(C_t > h\), where \(k\) is the reference value and \(h\) the decision threshold (Unkel et al. 2012). Accumulating over many weeks, CUSUM is more sensitive than Serfling to small, sustained shifts. But it does not handle seasonality itself: without a baseline model to standardise the data (Serfling’s residuals, for example), it raises false alarms every time the peak season begins.

6.7 Points to consider before applying it

Serfling assumes a clear seasonality that repeats steadily from year to year, with a low period long enough to anchor the baseline (Unkel et al. 2012). That usually holds for influenza in temperate regions, with one winter peak a year, but not everywhere. Before applying the method to a disease, indicator or area, check four points.

  1. A low period to anchor the baseline. If a disease circulates all year, cutting out calendar weeks loses its basis, and what remains may still hold epidemic activity. Feng and colleagues, estimating influenza-associated mortality in China, used Serfling only for northern cities with a clear winter peak; in the subtropical south influenza circulated all year (Feng et al. 2012).
  2. The shape of the season. Several peaks, asymmetric peaks or patterns that differ between regions may need more than one harmonic, so test \(r\) on each area’s own data. In northern Vietnam influenza can peak several times a year, in different seasons (Horby et al. 2012), and its seasonality in Vietnam is weak overall (Servadio et al. 2023).
  3. A stable peak timing. The model gives every year the same wave with a fixed phase. If the peak moves a lot between years, an average baseline fits no year well, and last year’s timing says little about next year’s. In Vietnam the interval between influenza peaks varies much more than in temperate regions (Servadio et al. 2023).
  4. Performance in context. Figures such as the high specificity in the French study come from one disease, indicator and surveillance system and may not carry over. Re-evaluate the method on the historical series of the disease and area to be monitored before using it in operation.

Summary

  • Serfling models the baseline as a long-term trend plus a Fourier series and sets the threshold about 1.645 standard deviations of the forecast error above it.
  • Each harmonic is a cosine–sine pair, equivalent to one wave with an amplitude and a phase. The analyst chooses \(r\) by testing; the data decide the coefficients.
  • Cleaning the history is the decisive step: left in, past epidemics push the threshold too high. Each cleaning method fails in its own way, so test it on the series to be monitored.
  • Requiring two consecutive weeks above the threshold reduces false alarms.
  • The Poisson variant (such as EuroMOMO) suits counts; Farrington and CUSUM complement Serfling when counts are small or an epidemic rises slowly.
  • In tropical regions, check the assumptions about seasonality first.
