6  Serfling

6.1 Overview

Every method so far has answered one question: is this week unusual for this time of year? The Farrington chapter ended on the question that kind of method cannot answer — has the epidemic season started? — because a normal seasonal rise is, by construction, not unusual for its time of year. Serfling’s method answers that second question. It models what the year would look like with no epidemic in it at all, and treats anything well above that line as epidemic activity.

A tide table works the same way. It predicts the water level from a handful of regular cycles, each with a fixed period, and a storm surge is whatever the table did not predict. Serfling’s baseline is a tide table for a disease: a slow trend plus a few waves of fixed length, fitted to years with the epidemics taken out, so that an epidemic is whatever rises above it.

Robert E. Serfling published the method in 1963 at the US Communicable Disease Center (CDC), originally to monitor weekly deaths from pneumonia and influenza (P&I) in 108 US cities (Serfling 1963). He chose P&I deaths as the main quantitative indicator even though excess deaths lag influenza cases by about four weeks, because other sources such as case reports or school and work absences were not quantitative enough or were not available on a wide scale. It is a parametric periodic regression model, and sixty years on it remains one of the foundational methods for setting epidemic thresholds (Unkel et al. 2012).

For a seasonal disease, one fixed threshold for the whole year does not work. A threshold low enough to catch an epidemic outside the season raises alarms continually during the peak season, while a threshold high enough for the peak season misses epidemics outside it. Serfling solves this by letting the threshold change over time, following the seasonal baseline.

The method rests on three main ideas:

  • Modelling the baseline: combine a long-term trend with sine and cosine waves (a Fourier series) to reproduce the seasonal variation in the absence of epidemics.
  • Cleaning the training data: remove or downweight past epidemic periods before fitting the model, so that old epidemics do not pull the baseline up.
  • Threshold and alarm rule: set the threshold above the baseline by a margin measured in standard deviations of the error (usually 1.64–1.645 standard deviations), and confirm an epidemic only when the indicator stays above the threshold for at least two consecutive weeks (Serfling 1963).
NoteCore idea

Epidemic threshold = the expected level with no epidemic + a safety margin for random variation. The variants of Serfling differ mainly in how they estimate these two components.

6.2 Mathematical basis

6.2.1 The general equation

The Serfling model with \(r\) harmonics has the form (Serfling 1963):

\[ E\left(Y_t\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \sum_{i=1}^{r}\left[\gamma_i\cos\left(\frac{2\pi i t}{L}\right) + \delta_i\sin\left(\frac{2\pi i t}{L}\right)\right] \tag{6.1}\]

The trend need not be a straight line: a quadratic or cubic polynomial can be used when the data change nonlinearly (Pelat et al. 2007). In the original paper, Serfling wrote the equation as \(\hat{Y} = u + bt + \sum a_i\cos\theta + \sum b_i\sin\theta\), with \(\theta\) a linear function of \(t\) (Serfling 1963); this lesson uses the symbols \(\beta\), \(\gamma\) and \(\delta\) to avoid clashing with notation in later sections.

6.2.2 Fourier series, intuitively

A Fourier series rests on a simple observation: any curve that repeats every year can be assembled from sine and cosine waves of different frequencies, in the same way that a chord is built from single notes.

  • Harmonic 1 has period \(L\) (52 weeks): it rises and falls once a year.
  • Harmonic 2 has period \(L/2\) (26 weeks): it rises and falls twice a year.
  • Harmonic \(i\) rises and falls \(i\) times a year.

At each harmonic, the cosine–sine pair is equivalent to a single wave with a given amplitude and phase:

\[ \gamma_i\cos\left(\frac{2\pi i t}{L}\right) + \delta_i\sin\left(\frac{2\pi i t}{L}\right) = A_i\cos\left(\frac{2\pi i t}{L} - \theta_i\right),\qquad A_i = \sqrt{\gamma_i^2 + \delta_i^2} \]

Here \(A_i\) is the amplitude (how tall the wave is) and \(\theta_i\) the phase (when the peak falls). With the cosine alone, the peak always sits at the start of the cycle; with the sine alone, it always sits a quarter of a cycle later. Mixing the two components in the ratio \(\gamma_i : \delta_i\) lets the peak fall in any week (Figure 6.1). The important benefit is that \(\gamma_i\) and \(\delta_i\) enter the model linearly, so they can be estimated by ordinary linear regression instead of estimating the phase by nonlinear regression.

Two points to remember:

  • The analyst chooses only \(L\) and \(r\). The coefficients \(\gamma_i\) and \(\delta_i\) (and so the amplitude and phase) are decided by the data, through the regression.
  • The frequency of each harmonic is fixed. The data decide only how tall that wave is and where its peak falls.

The first-order model (\(r = 1\)) gives exactly one symmetric peak a year:

\[ Y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \gamma_1\cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{52}\right) + \delta_1\sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{52}\right) + \varepsilon_t \]

The second-order model (\(r = 2\)) adds a semi-annual wave, allowing two peaks a year or one asymmetric peak that rises fast and falls slowly (Figure 6.1):

\[ Y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \gamma_1\cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{52}\right) + \delta_1\sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{52}\right) + \gamma_2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{26}\right) + \delta_2\sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{26}\right) + \varepsilon_t \]

Increasing \(r\) lets the curve fit more complex shapes (sharp peaks, several peaks). But if it is increased too far, the model fits random noise and leftover epidemic peaks as well. So \(r\) must be chosen by a statistical test, not by eye.

6.2.3 Choosing the number of harmonics: the example in the original paper

Serfling (1963) fitted the model to P&I deaths in 108 US cities over three seasons without a major epidemic (leaving out the 1957–58 pandemic season), with the data aggregated into thirteen four-week periods a year (Serfling 1963). To decide whether a second harmonic was needed, he used Bliss’s analysis of variance (a partial F test). The additional variance explained by adding harmonic 2 (mean square 8,136) was smaller than the residual variance of the second-order model (13,157), so \(F \approx 0.62 < 1\). Harmonic 2 carried no information beyond the level of the noise, and the first-order model was kept:

\[ \hat{Y}_t = 300.5 + 2.1\, t + 97.6\cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{13} - 2.67\right) \tag{6.2}\]

Here \(t\) is measured in four-week periods, with origin \(t = 0\) at the midpoint of the period ending 24 September 1955. The equation is written in amplitude–phase form: the amplitude is 97.6 deaths per week, and the peak falls at \(t = 2.67 \times 13/2\pi \approx 5.5\), around February 1956, in the middle of winter (Figure 6.2). Today the equivalent test in R is anova(m1, m2); models can also be compared by AIC (Pelat et al. 2007).

6.3 Computing an epidemic threshold with Serfling

To put the equation into routine surveillance, the method goes through four steps (Figure 6.3). The goal throughout is to separate baseline variation from the noise left by past epidemics before the threshold is computed.

1. Clean the history. Remove or downweight past epidemics before fitting, so the baseline describes a year without them. Five ways are in use:

  • By calendar. Drop seasons or weeks known to be epidemic. Serfling used only non-epidemic seasons and, when estimating the spread, replaced isolated high weeks with the trend value; when many years in a row had epidemics he estimated the trend from the low season alone, roughly mid-May to mid-September (Serfling 1963). EuroMOMO fits only spring and autumn weeks, when mortality is least affected by winter influenza and summer heat (EuroMOMO, n.d.).
  • By percentile. Drop a fixed share of the highest training weeks, typically about 15% (Pelat et al. 2007, 2017). On French influenza-like illness data the best configuration cut at the 86th percentile and used the full history (Souty et al. 2018).
  • Iteratively. Fit, drop points above a boundary — the fitted line in the first round, the upper 95% prediction limit after that — and refit, stopping when \(R^2\) stops improving (Wang et al. 2015).
  • Robustly. Keep every week but fit with iteratively reweighted estimation, such as MASS::rlm, so unusual weeks lose weight on their own (Pelat et al. 2017). The CDC used periodic regression with a robust procedure for its P&I baseline (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 1998).
  • As censored. Treat each epidemic week as left-censored — without the epidemic it would have been at most what was observed — and mark the week with the largest positive residual as epidemic, one at a time, up to a set share, about 16% for acute respiratory infection (Procházka and Kynčl 2016).

2. Fit and extrapolate. Estimate the coefficients on the cleaned data, by least squares or a weighted or likelihood variant, and project \(\hat Y_t\) forward over the weeks to be monitored. That projection is the seasonal baseline: what the community should expect if nothing epidemic is going on. Five years of training is common (EuroMOMO, n.d.; Pelat et al. 2017), but it is a choice to be tested; on the French data the full history did better than windows of 3, 5 or 10 years (Souty et al. 2018).

3. Set the threshold. Even with no epidemic the series scatters around the baseline, so the threshold sits a multiple of the forecast error above it:

\[ \text{Threshold}_t = \hat Y_t + z_\alpha\,\hat\sigma_t, \]

where \(\hat\sigma_t\) is the standard deviation of the forecast error, estimated from the residuals and including the extra uncertainty of extrapolating. Serfling and the CDC use \(z = 1.64\) to \(1.645\) (Serfling 1963; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 1998); Serfling corrected the standard deviation for the extrapolated trend but not for the Fourier terms. That \(z\) is the upper end of a one-sided 95% prediction interval — equivalently a two-sided 90% one, which is level = 0.90 in R’s predict() (McConeghy, n.d.). Santé publique France uses the two-sided 95% limit, \(z \approx 1.96\), trading sensitivity for specificity (Pelat et al. 2017). On a log-normal model the threshold is computed on the log scale and transformed back, \(\exp(\hat\mu_t + z_\alpha\hat\sigma)\) (Procházka and Kynčl 2016).

4. Require two weeks. A single week above the threshold is not yet an epidemic; one high week is as often a reporting delay or a data error as anything else. The onset is declared only when the series stays above the threshold two weeks in a row (Serfling 1963; Wang et al. 2015).

6.3.1 What cleaning does

Step 1 decides the quality of the whole method. Fit raw history and every past epidemic pulls the baseline up and swells the residual spread, so the threshold lands too high and new epidemics are flagged late (Unkel et al. 2012). The figure below simulates five training seasons, each with a winter epidemic, and a sixth season to monitor; because the data are simulated, the true baseline is known, and so is the real false alarm rate of any threshold.

Untrimmed, the threshold barely fires and the epidemic is confirmed one week before it peaks. Trim too hard and the normal seasonal peak goes too, so an ordinary week crosses the threshold almost one time in five. The right share is roughly the share of weeks that are actually epidemic — about 20% here; the common 15% was tuned on French influenza-like illness (Souty et al. 2018). Trimming also matters for choosing \(r\): untrimmed, the test for a second harmonic gives \(F = 27.8\), because the extra wave is chasing epidemic peaks; at 15% it falls to 1.6.

6.3.2 Why two weeks in a row

With \(z = 1.645\), an ordinary week still crosses the threshold about 5% of the time, on average 2–3 weeks a year (Serfling 1963). Over a 26-week winter–spring with no epidemic and independent weeks, the chance that at least one crosses is \(1 - 0.95^{26} = 0.736\). Requiring two weeks in a row leaves 25 adjacent pairs, each crossing with probability \(0.05^2\), so the chance falls to at most \(25 \times 0.0025 = 0.0625\) — exactly 0.058; Serfling quoted about one in ten.

The price is time: the chance that a genuine rise is still unflagged at the end of its second week is 0.9 for a moderate epidemic and 0.7 for a large one, and at the end of the third, 0.1 and under 0.01 (Serfling 1963). The arithmetic also assumes independent weeks. Serfling noted they are slightly correlated; at a correlation of 0.4 the two-week false alarm rate triples to about 18% (Figure 6.4).

6.3.3 Excess burden

Once the epidemic period has been identified, the model also estimates the excess burden, in one of two ways:

  • Against the baseline: \(\text{Excess}_t = Y_t - \hat{Y}_t\), summed over the epidemic weeks (Serfling 1963).
  • Against the threshold (more conservative): \(\text{Excess}_t = \max\left(Y_t - \text{Threshold}_t,\ 0\right)\), counting only the part above the threshold (McConeghy, n.d.).

6.4 Variants and practical implementation

6.4.1 P&I mortality surveillance at the US CDC

The CDC applied Serfling to the percentage of all deaths due to pneumonia and influenza. Using a percentage instead of an absolute count reduces bias when the number of reporting units changes from week to week. The baseline was projected by periodic regression combined with a robust procedure, and the epidemic threshold was set at 1.645 standard deviations above the baseline (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 1998). From October 2016, the data source moved from the 122 Cities Mortality Reporting System to the mortality surveillance system of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2016).

6.4.2 Serfling–Poisson and EuroMOMO

The OLS model assumes normally distributed residuals with constant variance. That assumption is reasonable when counts are large, but fits poorly with small or sparse counts, or with counts stratified by age group and small area (Unkel et al. 2012). The solution is to move the Serfling equation into a generalised linear model (GLM) with a Poisson distribution corrected for overdispersion (quasi-Poisson) and a log link:

\[ \log\left(\mu_t\right) = \log\left(N_t\right) + \eta_0 + \eta_1 t + \eta_2\sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{52}\right) + \eta_3\cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{52}\right) \]

Here \(\mu_t\) is the expected count and \(\log\left(N_t\right)\) an offset for population. The offset is included when population data are available, so that the model estimates a rate rather than a raw count.

This is the structure of the EuroMOMO algorithm (European monitoring of excess mortality): a Poisson GLM corrected for overdispersion, with a linear trend and an annual sine wave, fitted on the spring and autumn weeks of up to five previous years, plus a step that corrects for delay in death registration (EuroMOMO, n.d.). Two extensions are worth noting:

  • FluMOMO puts the level of influenza activity and extreme temperatures directly into the model as explanatory variables (Nielsen et al. 2018).
  • The modulation model lets the trend and the seasonal amplitude change smoothly over time through P-splines, instead of being fixed across years (Leger et al. 2025).

6.4.3 Implementation in R

WarningA note on using fluserf

In the current source code, y0_ul is computed as y0 + 1.645 × se.fit, the standard error of the fitted line rather than the forecast error. The resulting threshold is therefore narrower than the threshold as the CDC defines it. Check it before using it in operation.

A minimal example of the Serfling OLS model with percentile trimming and the two-week rule:

# df: weekly data with a column y (cases); the first 260 weeks are used for training
df$t  <- seq_len(nrow(df))
train <- df[df$t <= 260, ]
clean <- train[train$y <= quantile(train$y, 0.85), ]   # drop the highest 15% of weeks

m1 <- lm(y ~ t + cos(2*pi*t/52) + sin(2*pi*t/52), data = clean)
m2 <- update(m1, . ~ . + cos(4*pi*t/52) + sin(4*pi*t/52))
anova(m1, m2)                                           # is harmonic 2 needed?

new  <- df[df$t > 260, ]
pred <- predict(m1, newdata = new, interval = "prediction", level = 0.90)
new$baseline  <- pred[, "fit"]
new$threshold <- pred[, "upr"]                          # baseline + 1.645 x forecast error
over          <- new$y > new$threshold
new$alarm     <- over & c(FALSE, head(over, -1))        # above the threshold 2 weeks running

6.5 Comparison with other methods

In essence, Serfling works like a Shewhart control chart: each week is compared with the threshold independently. This catches sudden surges well but is less sensitive to slow, smouldering rises (Unkel et al. 2012). This section compares Serfling with three commonly used groups of methods.

6.5.1 The Moving Epidemic Method (MEM)

MEM was developed by Vega and colleagues for influenza surveillance in Europe (Vega et al. 2013). Instead of one wave function for the whole year, MEM divides each past season into three periods (pre-epidemic, epidemic and post-epidemic) based on the maximum accumulated rate percentage (MAP) curve. The epidemic threshold for a new season is the upper limit of a one-sided confidence interval computed from the highest values in the pre-epidemic periods of previous seasons.

On influenza-like illness data from the French Sentinelles network, compared against a gold standard set by expert consensus, Souty and colleagues found (Souty et al. 2018) (Figure 6.5):

  • Serfling (with percentile trimming) and MEM detected the epidemic start week equally well.
  • Serfling had higher specificity and positive predictive value, while MEM had higher sensitivity.
  • Robust regression detected onsets less well and was the only method to split one epidemic season into two waves.

6.5.2 The Farrington algorithm

The Farrington algorithm (Farrington et al. 1996) and Noufaily’s improved version (Noufaily et al. 2013) are widely used in routine surveillance in Europe and are available in R’s surveillance package (Salmon et al. 2016). Unlike Serfling, Farrington handles seasonality through the design of its reference window: to compute the threshold for week \(t_0\), the algorithm takes only the data within \(\pm w\) weeks of the same point in time in the \(b\) previous years (for example \(w = 3\), \(b = 5\)), and then fits a log-linear quasi-Poisson model:

\[ \log\left(\mu_t\right) = \theta_0 + \theta_1 t,\qquad Var\left(y_t\right) = \phi\,\mu_t \]

Past epidemics are downweighted based on their residuals, and the threshold is computed after a 2/3 power transformation to approximate a normal distribution, even for small counts:

\[ U_{t_0} = \hat{\mu}_{t_0}\left\{1 + \frac{2}{3}\, z_\alpha\,\frac{\left[\hat{\phi}\,\hat{\mu}_{t_0} + var\left(\hat{\mu}_{t_0}\right)\right]^{1/2}}{\hat{\mu}_{t_0}}\right\}^{3/2} \]

Thanks to its quasi-Poisson structure and local window, Farrington is better suited than classical Serfling to diseases with low or sparse counts. Its limitation is that it does not account for serial autocorrelation (Unkel et al. 2012).

6.5.3 The cumulative sum control chart (CUSUM)

CUSUM accumulates the positive standardised deviations over consecutive weeks:

\[ z_t = \frac{y_t - \mu_t}{\sigma_t},\qquad C_t = \max\left(0,\ C_{t-1} + z_t - k\right) \]

and raises an alarm when \(C_t > h\), where \(k\) is the reference value and \(h\) the decision threshold (Unkel et al. 2012). Because it accumulates information over many weeks, CUSUM is more sensitive than Serfling to small but sustained shifts. However, standard CUSUM does not handle seasonality on its own. If it is applied directly to seasonal data without standardising them with a baseline model (for example, using Serfling’s residuals), it raises false alarms every time the peak season begins.

6.6 Points to consider before applying it

Serfling’s method rests on a premise about the indicator being monitored: the disease has a clear seasonality that repeats steadily from year to year, and there is a low baseline period long enough to anchor the estimate of the baseline (Unkel et al. 2012). The premise usually holds for diseases with regular seasonality, such as influenza in temperate regions with one winter peak a year, but it is not always true. Before applying Serfling to a specific disease, indicator or area, four points need to be considered.

  1. There must be a low baseline period to anchor the estimate. If a disease circulates all year round without any truly “non-epidemic” period, cutting out a fixed band of calendar weeks to obtain the baseline loses its basis, and the remaining data may still contain epidemic activity. For example, when estimating influenza-associated mortality in China, Feng and colleagues used the Serfling model only for northern cities with a clear winter peak; in the subtropical southern cities, influenza circulated all year round without a distinct season (Feng et al. 2012).
  2. The seasonal shape may be more complex than one peak a year. Some diseases have several peaks a year, asymmetric peaks, or seasonal patterns that differ between regions. A first-order model (one peak a year) may then be insufficient, and the number of harmonics needs to be tested on each area’s own data. For example, in northern Vietnam influenza can produce several peaks in a year, falling in different seasons (Horby et al. 2012), and overall the seasonality of influenza in Vietnam is weak (Servadio et al. 2023).
  3. The timing of the peak needs to be relatively stable between years. The Serfling model uses the same seasonal wave, with a fixed phase, for every year. If the timing of the peak varies strongly from one year to the next, an “average” baseline built from previous years represents any particular year poorly, and the timing of last year’s peak says very little about the timing of next year’s. For example, in Vietnam the interval between influenza epidemic peaks varies much more than in temperate regions (Servadio et al. 2023).
  4. Performance depends on the data context. Performance figures, such as the high specificity in the French study, were measured on one disease, one indicator and one surveillance system, so they cannot be assumed to carry over to another context. Before putting the method into operation, it needs to be re-evaluated on the historical series of the very disease and area to be monitored.

Summary

  • Serfling estimates the expected baseline with a long-term trend plus a Fourier series, then sets the threshold about 1.645 standard deviations of the forecast error above the baseline.
  • Each Fourier harmonic is a cosine–sine pair, equivalent to one wave with an amplitude and a phase. The analyst chooses the number of harmonics \(r\) by testing; the coefficients are decided by the data.
  • Cleaning the historical data is the decisive step: if past epidemics are not removed, the threshold is set too high.
  • The rule of crossing the threshold for two consecutive weeks reduces false alarms.
  • The Poisson variant (such as EuroMOMO) is better suited to counts; Farrington and CUSUM complement Serfling when case numbers are small or an epidemic rises slowly.
  • In tropical regions, the assumptions about seasonality need to be checked again before the method is applied.
